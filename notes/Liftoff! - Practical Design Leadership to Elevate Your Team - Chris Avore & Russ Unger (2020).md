# Liftoff! Practical Design Leadership to Elevate Your Team
**Chris Avore & Russ Unger** | Rosenfeld Media, 2020

> A comprehensive field manual for newly-appointed design managers covering every phase from the first day in the role through scaling multi-team organizations, arguing that intentional systems — for hiring, critique, influence, and culture — are what separate thriving design teams from reactive ones. It matters because most designers who become managers receive no formal preparation, and this book closes that gap with concrete, immediately actionable frameworks.

---

## Chapter 1: Surprise, You're in Charge

The book opens with a frank reckoning: most design managers are promoted without warning, training, or a clear job description — and the skills that made them great designers have limited transfer value. The pivot required is from doing the work to enabling others to do the work.

- The design manager role sits at the intersection of design leadership and people management — neither fully one nor the other
- Managers must shift their identity from maker to multiplier; success is now measured through the team's output, not personal output
- "Design leader" is distinct from "design manager" — leadership is about vision, influence, and culture; management is about systems, process, and accountability
- The best early move is to listen aggressively: understand what the team needs before trying to change anything

> "You will not be evaluated on the quality of your own designs. You will be evaluated on the quality of the designs your team produces."

## Chapter 2: Designing Diversity and Inclusion

D&I is framed not as an HR compliance exercise but as a design problem: if the team doesn't represent the people it designs for, the outputs will have blind spots that no amount of user research can fully compensate for.

- Homogeneous teams have shared blind spots that are invisible from within — only outsiders can see them
- Inclusive hiring requires examining the full pipeline: job description language, sourcing channels, interview panel composition, and evaluation criteria
- Psychological safety is a prerequisite for inclusion — people can only contribute fully when they believe it's safe to speak up, disagree, and make mistakes
- Representation at the table is necessary but not sufficient; inclusive culture means those voices are actually heard and acted upon

## Chapters 3–7: The Hiring System

These five chapters treat hiring as a design project with discrete phases: defining the role, sourcing candidates, screening, interviewing, and bringing new hires aboard. Avore and Unger argue that ad-hoc hiring produces ad-hoc teams.

- **Performance Profiles over Job Descriptions**: Define what success looks like in 30/60/90 days rather than listing requirements — this keeps the focus on outcomes, not credentials
- **Screening**: Phone screens should be structured to evaluate the same dimensions across all candidates; unstructured screens introduce bias and miss signal
- **Interviewing**: Multi-person panels with calibrated rubrics reduce individual bias and create a shared evaluation vocabulary
- **The debrief ritual matters**: Who speaks first in the debrief shapes the outcome — structured evaluation before open discussion prevents anchoring on the loudest voice
- **Onboarding as design**: The first 30 days set expectations, relationships, and cultural norms — invest in a structured onboarding plan, not a self-guided tour of the wiki

> "If you design a bad interview experience, you're already designing a bad employee experience."

## Chapter 8: Team Charters

A team charter is a living agreement about how the team operates — its purpose, norms, decision-making protocols, and communication expectations. Avore and Unger present it as the foundational culture document every design team should build together.

- Charters are most powerful when co-created by the team rather than handed down by the manager — participation generates ownership
- Key sections: team purpose, operating principles, meeting cadences, decision rights, and conflict resolution norms
- Revisit and revise the charter when the team changes significantly — new members should help reshape it, not just inherit it
- The process of building a charter is as valuable as the artifact itself — it surfaces assumptions and disagreements before they become problems

## Chapter 9: One-on-Ones

One-on-ones are the single highest-leverage management tool available — a recurring, private, agenda-free space for the manager-report relationship to develop. Avore and Unger treat them as a discipline, not a check-in.

- The 1:1 belongs to the report, not the manager — come with your agenda, ask about theirs, and resist the pull to fill the time with status updates
- Four conversation types: career development, current work, relationship-building, and feedback — rotate intentionally rather than defaulting to the same type every week
- The most important signal is what the report chooses to bring — patterns over time reveal their priorities, anxieties, and ambitions more honestly than any survey
- Canceling 1:1s signals that they're low-priority; consistency is itself a management message

## Chapter 10: Continuous Critique

Critique is the primary mechanism through which design quality improves — and most organizations do it badly. Avore and Unger distinguish critique (structured feedback toward goals) from criticism (judgment) and approval-seeking (validation).

- Establish the goal of the critique before any work is shown — feedback must be anchored to something, or it defaults to personal preference
- The presenter's job is to frame the work; the critic's job is to respond to that frame — both roles require preparation
- Regular, low-stakes critique is healthier than high-stakes milestone reviews; frequent small adjustments beat infrequent large corrections
- Critique culture is a team culture — if the manager only receives positive feedback, the team isn't safe enough to critique upward

> "Critique is a gift. It takes time, attention, and care. Treat it accordingly."

---

## Chapter 11: Presenting Work

How work is presented shapes how it is received. Avore and Unger argue that presentation is a design skill that most designers underdevelop because formal education rarely teaches it.

- Frame the problem before showing the solution — audiences can't evaluate a solution they don't understand the problem for
- Anticipate the questions and objections; address them proactively in the narrative rather than reactively in Q&A
- Adapt the presentation to the audience's context: executives need the top-line conclusion first; peers need the reasoning
- Practice is not optional for high-stakes presentations — even experienced presenters benefit from a run-through with a friendly audience

---

## Chapter 12: Saying No

Design managers are constantly asked for more than they can deliver — more features, faster timelines, broader scope. Saying no effectively is a professional skill that protects the team's capacity and the work's quality.

- "No" is more credible when accompanied by an alternative — "not now, but here's what we can do" lands differently than a flat refusal
- Prioritization frameworks (Impact vs. Effort, RICE, etc.) make no-decisions feel principled rather than arbitrary — use them publicly
- Saying yes to everything signals that the team's commitments aren't real, which destroys trust in timelines and estimates
- Protect the team from context-switching — each addition to the plate reduces throughput on everything already there

---

## Chapter 13: Developing Designers

Manager-as-coach is the mode that scales; manager-as-doer does not. Avore and Unger provide frameworks for skill mapping, career laddering, delegation, and performance review that turn development into a system rather than a hope.

- Skill mapping against a career ladder makes development conversations concrete — "here's where you are, here's where the next level is"
- Delegation should be calibrated to stretch the designer without overwhelming them — the goal is growth, not either comfort or crisis
- Performance reviews should contain no surprises — if feedback has been given continuously, the review is a summary, not a revelation
- The most powerful development tool is the right assignment: putting someone in a role slightly above their current capability, with support

---

## Chapter 14: Scaling Design

As design teams grow, the systems that worked at five people break at twenty. Avore and Unger walk through the operational, cultural, and structural changes that growth demands.

- Design operations (DesignOps) emerges at scale to handle tooling, process, resourcing, and measurement — it's not overhead, it's infrastructure
- Culture becomes harder to transmit as the team grows; document and ritualize what used to be ambient
- Org model decisions (centralized, federated, embedded) each have trade-offs; the right answer depends on the maturity of the organization and the nature of the product
- Systems thinking at scale: design systems, critique systems, hiring systems — the design manager becomes a systems designer for the team itself

---

## Chapter 15: Designing Influence

Influence without authority is the core challenge of design leadership — most of what matters happens in rooms the design manager wasn't invited to, and the only currency is trust and credibility built over time.

- Influencing up requires speaking the language of the people above you — business outcomes, risk, timeline, not design craft
- Build relationships across functions before you need them — influence is a long-term investment, not a short-term negotiation tactic
- Culture change is design work: identify the current state, define the desired state, and prototype interventions to close the gap
- Visibility of design work inside the organization is a prerequisite for influence — people can't value what they don't see

> "Your job is not to be right. Your job is to make the right thing happen."

---

## Chapter 16: Escape Velocity

The closing chapter frames the whole arc: design leadership is a continuous practice, not a destination. Avore and Unger end with an honest inventory of what the job demands and what it gives back.

- Leadership requires constant self-assessment — the same curiosity and iteration designers apply to products must be applied to their own practice
- The goal is escape velocity: getting the team to a place where it can sustain and improve itself without constant intervention
- Building the next generation of leaders is both the hardest and most important work a design leader does
- Design leadership at its best is a force multiplier — one skilled leader enables dozens of designers to do their best work
