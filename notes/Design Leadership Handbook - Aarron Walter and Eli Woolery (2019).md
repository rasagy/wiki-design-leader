# Design Leadership Handbook
**Aarron Walter and Eli Woolery** | DesignBetter.Co, 2019

> A practical guide for designers making the leap from individual contributor to leader, arguing that the skills that made you a great designer are necessary but insufficient for effective leadership. It matters because the transition is widely mishandled — and this book gives you the frameworks to do it deliberately.

---

## Chapter 1: Becoming a Design Leader

The move into leadership strips away the craft work that earned you the role, replacing it with a new and uncomfortable set of responsibilities that require entirely different skills. The authors use Andy Law (Netflix) as a model: he researched the role before accepting it, studied failures as much as successes, and entered it with the mindset "I'll never be 100% prepared, but I'll always be 100% committed."

- Individual contributor skills — pushing pixels, executing on craft — do not transfer to management; you must deliberately build a new skillset
- As a leader, you conduct the orchestra rather than play an instrument; your medium shifts from design to the environment and structure that produces design
- Designers accustomed to introverted, qualitative work face real emotional challenges in executive contexts where "this feels right" must become "this meets our business goals"
- The core responsibilities of design leadership: building the team, managing individuals, operationalizing process, forging alliances, and shaping vision

> "I'm never going to be 100% prepared to be a design leader, but I'll always be 100% committed." — Andy Law, Netflix

## Chapter 2: Building the Team

The most important job a design leader has is hiring — the people you bring in form your team's foundation, carry your culture forward, and outlast your tenure. Chemistry and complementary skills matter as much as individual talent.

- Define team values collaboratively (using sticky notes, dot voting) before hiring, so culture is explicit rather than accidental — "culture fit" hiring should be avoided in favor of "culture add"
- Prioritize soft skills over technical gaps; the primary reasons for letting people go are almost always soft-skill failures, and technical gaps can be coached
- Look for: broad perspectives from diverse backgrounds, adaptation and grit (Duckworth's framework), collaborative mindset, humility balanced with confidence
- Three team structures to consider: **Centralized** (agency model, good for craft culture and consistency), **Embedded/EPD** (Engineering-Product-Design cross-functional, good for speed and empathy), and **Hybrid** (context-dependent blend)
- Never leave a designer solo on a cross-functional team — pair them or risk stagnation
- Fast hiring leads to slow, painful firings; a toxic team member poisons the whole group

> "Hand out priorities, not tasks, and let your people be creative about their own execution." — Kavin Stewart, Reddit

## Chapter 3: Managing a Design Team

A design manager is a servant leader whose primary job is managing the careers of others — not reviewing pixels. Regular 1-on-1s, recognition rituals, and matching the right designer to the right project type are the core mechanics of good management.

- 1-on-1s are not status updates; they are the only place to build rapport, surface career goals, and give candid feedback in both directions — use tools like 15five to keep them focused
- Recognize and celebrate milestones explicitly; moving to the next project without acknowledging completion leaves teams feeling empty
- Two designer archetypes: **Hunters** (thrive on ambiguity, new products, open-ended exploration) and **Farmers** (thrive on constraints, iteration, and refinement) — mismatching them to project types causes chaos
- Introduce management layers gradually: assign one direct report, observe, then expand — never use pay raises as the incentive for moving into management (it attracts the wrong people)
- Resolve conflicts early, gather information individually from all parties, then bring everyone into a room for a structured conversation before things fester

> "I used to think that if you cared for other people you need to study sociology… I concluded if you want to help other people, be a manager." — Clay Christensen, Harvard Business School

## Chapter 4: Operationalizing Design

A healthy design culture requires structured feedback rituals — not as bureaucracy, but as the mechanism by which junior designers grow, senior designers stay accountable, and projects don't drift undetected. The physical and digital environment should be designed to invite feedback constantly.

- Post work-in-progress on studio walls daily; lower fidelity signals openness and invites more candid feedback than polished comps
- Four core feedback formats: **Design Reviews** (detailed critique framed by project goals, 7 people max), **Design Standups** (daily 3-question sync, keep it short and standing), **Retrospectives** (after every project: What worked / What didn't / What to improve), **Postmortems** (after failures: impartial facilitator, focus on events not blame, actions with owners and deadlines)
- Remote teams can replicate this with tools like InVision LiveShare, Slack, and Trello — distributed is not an excuse for skipping feedback culture
- Send a pre-retrospective survey individually before the meeting to prevent the bandwagon effect from suppressing minority views
- Feedback becomes easier over time as psychological safety grows — start early and make it a habit

> "You know you have a healthy design culture when people are giving each other feedback." — Dustin Senos, formerly Medium

## Chapter 5: Forging Alliances

Design leaders cannot operate in isolation — the design team is one piece of a larger ecosystem, and the success of your work depends on the social capital you build with engineering, product, research, support, and executive stakeholders. Visibility and inclusion are strategic acts.

- Get out from behind your computer: grab lunch with engineers, sit in on research sessions, attend sales calls — every team has context that makes your designs better
- Invite engineers and executives into design reviews deliberately; it keeps design visible and builds cross-functional empathy without waiting to be discovered
- Post work in physical spaces so passersby can give spontaneous, unsolicited feedback — print is persistent and casual in ways digital is not
- Educate the company about design through UX Progressives (desk demos), film screenings (e.g., DESIGN DISRUPTORS), office hours, lunch-and-learns, and dedicated Slack channels
- Stakeholder inclusion is not design by committee — it's a way to surface good ideas early and convert potential critics into invested collaborators

> "Your legs are your most effective design tool. Get out and connect with people." — Mark Opland, Facebook

## Chapter 6: Shaping Design Vision

Without a compelling "why," product teams optimize for milestones rather than meaning — roadmaps tell you what to build, but only story tells you why. The design leader's job is to craft and sustain a North Star that orients every team.

- Product roadmaps show what and when; design vision shows *why* — without the latter, teams lose their mission as scope grows and sprints accelerate
- Story is the most effective medium for vision: short videos, storyboards, comics, or illustrated boards all work — what matters is that they show how the product fits into real lives, not just what it does
- MailChimp's UX team created a vision video in ~10 days before a 4-month redesign; Airbnb worked with Pixar illustrators for storyboards — the medium is less important than the clarity it forces
- Design leaders don't have to choose between craft and strategy (Don Norman's "When you come to a fork in the road, take it") — staying close to making strengthens the thinking

> "People don't buy what you do; they buy why you do it." — Simon Sinek (cited by authors)
