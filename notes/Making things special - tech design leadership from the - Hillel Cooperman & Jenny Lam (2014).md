# Making Things Special: Tech Design Leadership from the Trenches
**Hillel Cooperman & Jenny Lam** | Jackson Fish Market Publishing, 2014

> A candid, opinionated battle manual for designers working in engineering-dominated technology organizations, arguing that true UX leadership means owning the entire product experience — not just the pixels — and that designers must actively fight for organizational authority, not wait to be granted it. It matters because most software companies still subordinate design to engineering and product management, and this book gives designers the language and tactics to change that.

---

## Chapters 1–5: The Philosophy of UX (What We're Actually Doing Here)

The opening chapters establish the authors' foundational beliefs about what software design is and why most organizations get it wrong. The Bill Gates story (Ch1) anchors the whole book: even a brilliant technologist can be blind to the emotional and relational dimensions of software design, because the industry's currency is the engineer.

- The line between software and content is no longer relevant — software must tell a story and create an emotional connection, not just solve a problem
- The automobile industry's history is the template: Ford resisted design; General Motors hired Harley Earl and built the Art and Color Section; GM surpassed Ford as the world's largest manufacturer — technology companies are making this same transition now
- Software is both art and craft, not one or the other — the Empire State Building is crafted to serve function AND inspires awe; great software should do both
- The history of GUI (Mac 1984 → Web 1990s → Mobile 2000s) shows each era added new imperatives: first consistency, then discoverability, then brand and emotional connection

> "The line between software and content isn't just blurry, it's irrelevant. But its continued existence makes many technologists eschew the foundational design and storytelling techniques that are critical for making great user experiences."

## Chapters 6–9: Usability, Branding, and Success Metrics

These chapters argue for raising the bar beyond "usable" — a baseline, not a destination — and honestly confront the difficulty of measuring UX success.

- Usability is the new minimum bar: it's like saying a restaurant's food is "edible" — baseline, not aspirational. The space above usability is emotional experience and brand
- Brand is not a logo — it's "a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or organization" (Marty Neumeier) — and designers own far more of brand formation than they realize, since customers spend far more time with the product than with ads
- MailChimp is the exemplar: brand values (fun, casual, powerful) expressed throughout every interaction, including the chimp's arm that rips off when your email is too wide
- Defining success in major UX redesigns is genuinely hard: measurements are muddy, user complaints are vocal and unrepresentative, and analytics are rarely built correctly — accept the ambiguity, focus on a few key metrics, and trust big-picture indicators (press, employee morale, customer satisfaction)
- **On luck**: most business books ignore it, but luck may account for upwards of 75% of success — "you don't win the lottery if you don't buy a ticket," so designing great software buys you tickets

## Chapters 10–15: The Organizational Politics of Design

The most distinctive section of the book — frank, provocative analysis of how designers should navigate their relationships with engineers, marketers, and product managers.

- The ideal UX designer is a "singer-songwriter" — someone who both composes (interaction design, product definition) and performs (visual design, top-layer execution) — splitting these into separate roles typically produces worse outcomes
- Silicon Valley's desire for "designers who can code" betrays a fundamental misunderstanding of what designers do — we don't want a great designer spending time fighting CSS browser inconsistencies
- **Designer vs. Engineer**: the relationship breaks when engineers have such low expectations of designers that they don't even expect basic technical awareness; the best partnerships happen when both sides bring craft and empathy to the collaboration
- **Designer vs. Marketer**: in engineering-led companies, designers can become marketers' biggest allies by doing design favors for them — build the relationship, and they become advocates for design inside the organization
- **Designer vs. Product Manager**: the modern UX design leader should own what Product Managers currently own — customer understanding, functionality definition, and product strategy — relegating PMs to project management and outbound marketing
- Don't declare yourself the "user advocate" — it implicitly excuses everyone else from caring about users, and sets up a defensive posture rather than a collaborative one

> "It must be the goal of every aspiring User Experience Designer to eliminate the need for Product Managers who do anything other than outbound marketing."

---

## Chapters 16–20: How to Lead, Hire, and Build Design Organizations

The final section turns practical: how to argue for design leadership, how to hire, and how to grow into the role when no external model exists.

- **Put the designer in charge**: the goal of a technology company isn't to write code or make money — it's to create a user experience that is indispensable, delightful, and special. The person in charge should be the person with the deepest expertise in creating that experience
- **How to hire a designer**: formal design training, strong portfolio (every piece should be great; any dud reflects poor judgment), passionate about technology, has shipped something, and above all: amazing attitude — curious, positive, humble, and eager to grow
- If you can't find a senior UX leader, grow one — identify someone who lives and breathes customer experience, build a small virtual team around them, and create conditions that let the junior designer earn leadership organically
- **Turning yourself into a UX leader**: understand the business metrics as well as the design metrics; speak the language of engineers, marketers, and executives; take on responsibilities beyond the brief
- **Destroying your enemies through the magic of design**: the most effective argument for design is always the work itself — make something so good that resistance becomes untenable

> "Every product you put out, every experience you create, that is better designed, more customer-centric, and even more thoughtful, will raise the bar further, and create incentives for the rest of the industry to follow suit."
