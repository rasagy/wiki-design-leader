# Org Design for Design Orgs: Building and Managing In-House Design Teams
**Peter Merholz & Kristen Skinner** | O'Reilly Media, 2016

> A practitioner's framework for building, structuring, and maturing in-house design organizations in technology companies, arguing that most companies underinvest in design not from malice but from a lack of understanding of what design can actually do — and that the solution is to establish design as a peer to engineering and business, with its own leadership, authority, and accountability. It matters because the shift from products to services requires continuous design embedded throughout the organization, not a pixelated add-on at the end of development.

---

## Chapter 1: Why Design? Why Now?

The business case for design investment has never been stronger — and this chapter walks through the structural forces that made it so. The Intuit/Mint acquisition ($170M for what was essentially a better UX on commodity technology) is the opening proof point: design is now a competitive asset, not a finishing coat.

- Four forces converge to make design essential: the limits of efficiency optimization, "software is eating the world" (Andreessen), the consumerization of all software (users expect consumer-grade quality everywhere), and the shift to everything-as-a-service (which demands continuous design, not one-time delivery)
- Design is not just problem-solving — that framing is reductionist; design's full potential includes problem-framing, strategy, and the injection of humanism into organizations
- Companies that resist this evolution — treating design as a styling pass on finished engineering — will lose to competitors who understand design as a systemic capability
- IBM's 1000-designer hiring plan, McKinsey acquiring Lunar, Capital One and BBVA acquiring design firms: the boardroom has already absorbed this lesson

> "The best designed products and services don't simply solve problems, they connect deeply with people."

## Chapter 2: Realizing the Potential of Design

Design's role has expanded from execution (making the brief come to life) to definition (influencing what gets built) to strategy (shaping what the company pursues). This chapter maps that expanded mandate using the Double Diamond framework — discovery/definition then development/delivery — and argues that most organizations only engage design in the second diamond.

- "Design is the rendering of intent" (Jared Spool) — a definition broad enough to include strategy, not just craft
- The Double Diamond: design's value in the first diamond (strategy and definition) is deeply underutilized; low-fidelity sketches catch incoherent strategies early, when iteration is cheap — not during development, when changes are expensive
- Customer-centered planning (inspired by Henrik Kniberg's MVP diagram) means ensuring every iterative release delivers a complete, coherent experience — not a feature list that only coheres at the finish line
- The three-legged stool: product delivery now requires business, technology, and design in equal leadership — any leg deficient makes the offering wobble
- Bringing design in-house is now standard; outsourced design cannot keep pace with continuous delivery or internalize the company's values and context

## Chapter 3: 12 Qualities of Effective Design Organizations

The central contribution of the book — a diagnostic framework for assessing and evolving a design organization, organized into three groups: Foundation, Output, and Management. The Golden State Warriors analogy opens the chapter: talent isn't sufficient; the team that won the championship had the same roster as the team that lost in the first round — the difference was leadership and culture.

### Foundation

**1. Focused, empowered leadership** — A single, clear line of design authority (not a committee of peers). At scale, this can split into a Creative Director (vision and quality) and a VP of Design (operations and hiring), mirroring the CTO/VP Engineering model.

**2. Autonomy** — Leadership must have the authority to define how design operates, establish cross-functional working methods, and — critically — say no to work that isn't adequately prioritized or staffed.

**3. Executive access** — Design leadership must be one or two rungs from the CEO. Distance from the C-suite makes it easy for others to dismiss design's contribution and makes end-to-end experience work politically impossible.

**4. Authentic user empathy** — Beyond surveys and usability testing: field research, in-home visits, following people through their days. Quantitative methods reveal where things break; qualitative field research reveals why, and builds the empathy that makes design solutions actually land. A secondary benefit: when executives witness raw user research footage, they become design advocates.

**5. Understand, articulate, and create value** — Designers must speak the language of business outcomes: conversion rates, engagement, churn, brand. Shielding behind the cloak of 'creatives' loses credibility. When design proposes ways to unlock new business value — not just execute against it — its organizational standing grows.

### Output

**6. Support the entire journey** — One design organization should span the entire customer journey, from marketing through product through support. The historical split between "product design" and "marketing design" is a 20th-century manufacturing artifact that doesn't hold in a services world. Required skills span interaction design, information architecture, visual design, brand identity, service design, and more.

**7. Deliver at all levels of scale** — Design must operate from 10,000 feet (integrated company strategy) down to 1 foot (typography, spacing, transitions). A single designer can't span all levels; even early-stage hiring should bring in two designers with complementary scale-focus. Great design impact requires presence at the strategic level, not just the surface level.

**8. Establish and uphold quality standards** — Quality must be defined, externalized, and protected — not left to subjective taste. Design leaders must wield the power of "No": work should only proceed when adequately staffed and prioritized. The instinct to say yes to everything (to please, and to avoid shipping undesigned work) leads to teams spread too thin, delivering mediocrity across the board.

**9. Value delivery over perfection** — The counterbalance to quality: "Real artists ship." In a continuous delivery world, perfectionism is a liability. Delivery is a habit; the Big Launch mindset is paralyzing. Leadership must recognize the point of diminishing returns and push work into the world, where real users provide feedback that internal assessment never can.

### Management

**10. Teams are made of people, not resources** — Bureaucratic management treats employees as interchangeable widgets. Design leaders must know team members as individuals, support their idiosyncratic strengths, and refuse the false economies of overwork. Creative work requires rest, renewal, and space for growth — not 60-hour weeks and title-based equivalence.

**11. Diversity of perspective and background** — Homogeneous teams design for themselves. A team of people with the same background, training, and demographics will produce solutions that reflect those shared limitations. Gender and racial diversity remain persistent challenges in the field; addressing them requires pipeline work — engaging grade schools, partnering with organizations like Inneract Project — not just sourcing from the same design schools.

**12. Foster a collaborative environment** — Psychological safety is a prerequisite for honest critique, and honest critique is what makes design work great. Senior leaders must share their own work and receive critique with humility. Physical studio space — whiteboards, walls, project rooms — supports continuous lightweight check-ins rather than infrequent high-stakes reviews.

**13. Manage operations effectively** — The most underappreciated aspect of design leadership. Poor operations are often misread as creative dysfunction: file management chaos, unclear process, mismatched staffing, duplicate efforts, no measurement. Design teams that resist operations practices often do so because past processes were overcorrected into rigidity; the goal is smooth operations that enable focus on the actual work.

> "A design organization is only as good as what it delivers, and if it is producing crap because it's trying to do too many things, than the rest of the organization will associate design with crap."
