# Voices of Design Leadership: Insights from Top Collaborative Design Firms
**Ken Sanders FAIA** | John Wiley & Sons, 2022

> An interview-based study of sixteen diverse architectural design leaders across major global firms, arguing that thriving design organizations are built on cultural patterns and personal values — not operational formulas. It matters because the profession's leadership ranks still skew heavily toward white men, and Sanders uses these profiles to model what more inclusive, collaborative, and mission-driven leadership can look like.

---

## Part One: Patterns of Design Ecosystems

Healthy design firms share cultural patterns that are not rules or strategies but norms that grow over time — Sanders identifies six that appear across all the firms profiled, regardless of size, structure, or specialty.

- **Power of Diversity**: Diverse teams are more resilient and productive (Sanders draws an explicit parallel to biodiversity in ecosystems); DEI isn't just ethical, it improves design outcomes — "seek out, hire, and learn from people who are different from you"
- **Design + Business Synergy**: All firms live somewhere on a Practice-Centered Business ↔ Business-Centered Practice spectrum; neither extreme survives — the key is awareness of where you are and making conscious trade-offs
- **Client Experience Mindset**: Differentiation, not lower fees, is the answer to competitive pressure; "our fees were too high" almost always means "we failed to differentiate"
- **T-shaped Professionals**: Deep expertise in one discipline paired with broad collaborative fluency across many; leaders should hire for this shape intentionally
- **The Four R's**: (Recruit, Retain, Reward, and Replenish) — talent health is an ongoing practice, not a hiring event
- **Lifelong Learning**: All sixteen leaders share an explicit commitment to continuous learning — from formal programs (Gensler University) to mentorship to academic partnerships

## Part Two: Voices — Key Leaders and What They Teach

Rather than profiling all sixteen individually, the cross-cutting lessons from the conversations:

**On collaborative leadership style** — Sharron van der Meulen (ZGF) leads by listening first and working toward consensus rather than forcing decisions. Colin Koop (SOM) describes his role as "conductor — taking this great orchestra made up of so many individual talents, and making it work as seamlessly as possible." Mustafa Chehabbedine (KPF) sketches lightly and disposably so ideas stay in conversation rather than becoming fixed.

**On mission-driven practice** — Alan Ricks co-founded MASS Design Group as a 501(c)(3) non-profit ("Model of Architecture Serving Society"), beginning with a hospital in Rwanda. MASS draws inspiration from the slow food movement's "good, clean and fair" framework — applying it to design quality, environmental sustainability, and ethical supply chains. Marsha Maytum (Leddy Maytum Stacy) has built a firm centered on sustainability, equity, and mentorship — with osmotic learning in an open, horizontal studio as a deliberate culture design choice.

**On research as a leadership strategy** — Billie Faircloth (KieranTimberlake) leads an intentionally transdisciplinary research group spanning chemical physics, materials science, and architecture. She frames her work through innovation metrics rather than productivity metrics: "you trust the workflow and the process more than the metrics themselves." Michelle Kaufmann (Google) applies systems thinking and prefabrication R&D to compress design-to-construction timelines.

**On equity and diversity as leadership action** — Ana Pinto-Alexander (HKS) walked into her first leadership meeting and immediately challenged colleagues: "I guarantee you there are half a dozen remarkable women at HKS — why are they not sitting at the table here with me?" Andre Brumfield (Gensler) has spent his career working on equitable city-building, connecting urban design to racial and socioeconomic justice.

**On climate as a design imperative** — Margaret Montgomery (NBBJ) argues for designing beyond minimum compliance: hospitals being designed today won't open until 2028 — their embodied carbon footprint is the most significant near-term climate impact, not operational energy. Nader Tehrani (Cooper Union/NADAAA) pushes architecture education to transcend LEED and engage with the full ethical complexity of making.

**On personal leadership formation** — Rafael Viñoly describes immigration as "probably the only university that can teach you how much you don't know." Wendy Rogers (LPA) spent 30 years at the same firm, from intern to CEO, by continuously stretching into new challenges — the pandemic taught her "humility... the smartest thing I could do was share what I knew and admit what we didn't." Mariko Masuoka (Pelli Clarke) cautions young architects who don't realize a building takes five years from start to finish: "If you don't make a commitment to see it all the way through, you don't really get the full experience."

> "There is so much talent at SOM, and it is our challenge to try to match the talent with the design problem." — Colin Koop, SOM

## Part Three: Design Leadership Values

Across all sixteen leaders, Sanders distills six personal leadership values that sustain collaborative design ecosystems over time — distinct from professional values like sustainability or innovation.

- **Integrity and Trust**: It takes a long time to build trust and only one bad decision to unravel it; authenticity means your private values and public persona are the same — you treat people who can't do anything for you the same as you treat clients and executives
- **Equity and Inclusion**: Moving from awareness to action; multiple leaders describe actively restructuring hiring, promotion, and meeting dynamics to bring underrepresented voices to the table
- **Communication Biomimicry**: Effective design leaders adapt their communication style to context — like biological systems that use different signals for different environments — rather than applying one mode to all situations
- **Servant Leadership**: Authority deployed to clear obstacles and enable others, not to maintain hierarchy; modeled visibly so that teams internalize and replicate it
- **Informed Optimism**: Grounded in evidence and research, not wishful thinking — optimism as a discipline that enables action in the face of complexity and constraint
- **The Wild Card**: Sanders acknowledges that the most consequential leadership qualities are often the hardest to systematize — idiosyncratic strengths that emerge from a leader's specific background, biography, and risk tolerance

> "Helping others achieve success has been central to my leadership roles at multiple design firms and served as a key motivation for writing this book." — Ken Sanders

| **Biography as leadership asset** | Van der Meulen (listening as an introvert), Viñoly (immigration as education), Pinto-Alexander (immigrant background informing equity work), Kaufmann (prefab entrepreneur turned Google R&D director) |
| **Diversity as resilience, not compliance** | Ch2 (biodiversity analogy), Brumfield (equity cities), Pinto-Alexander (HKS leadership table), Koop (SOM talent + equity team), Montgomery (climate justice) |
| **Small team depth over large team breadth** | Faircloth (transdisciplinary research group), Maytum (horizontal studio), Masuoka (Pelli Clarke studio model), Ricks (200-person non-profit with outsized impact) |
| **Practice-business tension** | Ch2 (spectrum framework), Harrison (Perkins&Will tripled in size under his leadership), Ricks (non-profit model that "did everything wrong" by conventional metrics) |
| **Commitment to place and time** | Masuoka (see buildings through to completion), Rogers (30 years at one firm), Brumfield (decades of Chicago community work), Maytum (mission-driven practice as a long game) |
| **Learning as organizational design** | Sanders (Gensler University), Faircloth (research partnerships as learning), Tehrani (practice + academia integration), Maytum (osmotic studio mentorship) |
