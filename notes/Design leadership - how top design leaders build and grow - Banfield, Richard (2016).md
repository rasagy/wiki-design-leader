# Design Leadership: How Top Design Leaders Build and Grow
**Richard Banfield** | O'Reilly, 2016

> Based on ~100 interviews with design leaders across North America (companies ranging from 5 to 100+ employees), this book distills patterns from practitioners rather than prescribing a single playbook.

---

## Core Premise

Almost 50% of design leaders surveyed consider themselves "reasonable leaders still learning." Even the best are confused and making it up as they go. The goal of the book is to surface real strategies — not myths — from top digital design leaders.

---

## Chapter 1: Culture

**Central idea:** Culture is the #1 priority for successful design businesses, yet it can't be manufactured with perks — it comes from the people you hire and the values you embody.

- Leaders don't create culture; they create the *container* (vision + values) and fill it with the right people
- Culture trickles down from founders — their personality becomes company personality
- Healthy culture = a *learning culture* with a growth mindset
- Ping-pong tables and beanbags don't create culture; people do
- Culture directly drives staff retention and loyalty
- Team structure and composition shape culture — select and combine teammates carefully

> "Ultimately, as design leaders, we're building teams and companies, not products and things." — John Torres, America's Test Kitchen

---

## Chapter 2: Talent

**Central idea:** Building a talent pipeline is as important and ongoing as building a sales pipeline.

- Treat talent development like a sales pipeline — invest in it constantly
- Hire people smarter than you; hire for soft skills and train hard skills
- Diversity of skills and backgrounds adds creativity and wisdom
- Coachable people often outperform highly knowledgeable but rigid ones
- Hiring junior/inexperienced people is not always a cost saving
- Hire when capacity is *consistently* maxed out — not before
- Use freelancers to buffer business cycle fluctuations
- Apprenticeship programs can be both a talent pipeline and a profit center
- Most companies interviewed did *not* use external recruiters — strong reputation and internal programs did the work

**Key question for retention:** Why are you losing bright people? How does your culture need to evolve to keep them?

## Chapter 3: Office Space and Remote Working

**Central idea:** Physical space reflects and reinforces culture — there's no single right model, but intentionality matters.

- Office layout should balance open (collaboration) and closed (focus) spaces
- The company's brand and personality should show through in the space
- Moving people around the office periodically creates new connections
- When relocating, preserve the cultural elements that work
- Remote teams need frequent, *routine* communication to stay bonded
- Hire self-managers for remote roles — self-management is prerequisite, not bonus
- Even fully remote teams benefit from periodic in-person gatherings

## Chapter 4: Personal Growth and Finding Balance

**Central idea:** The design leader's personal health and growth are inseparable from the organization's health.

- Your team is your primary customer as a leader
- Having personal *and* professional purpose gives focus to growth
- Invest in people and processes to reduce operational distractions
- Actively develop soft skills (negotiation, conflict resolution, presentation) — these are perennially underdeveloped
- Use your calendar proactively to protect personal and family time
- Design your ideal life the same way you design for users — map your days, weeks, months, years
- Exercise is the primary stress-reduction tool cited by leaders (walking, running, cycling, yoga)
- Find partners and advisors who complement your weaknesses
- Life comes in waves — use slow periods to recharge

## Chapter 5: Planning for the Future

**Central idea:** Planning in an ambiguous environment requires flexible frameworks, not rigid roadmaps — and it's a team sport.

- Great plans start with a clear vision, guiding principles, values, and action steps
- Plans never survive contact with reality intact — build in flexibility
- Planning is not a solo activity: involve partners, advisors, mentors, and team
- Growth is not linear — plan for bursts of fast growth with slow periods between
- Long-term planning still matters despite fast-changing technology
- Use planning to stay focused and filter out distractions
- Connect plans to specific revenue outcomes to stay grounded

## Chapter 6: Leadership Styles

**Central idea:** There is no single ideal leadership style — but all effective styles ultimately motivate and empower the team.

- Embrace failure as feedback; leaders who fail frequently (and learn) outperform those who avoid failure
- Lead by example — get your hands dirty, but delegate intentionally
- Avoid micromanagement; instead return authority and build trust
- Protect one-on-ones — the feedback loop is too valuable to delegate
- Good leadership means being *of* the team while still providing direction
- Create an environment where failures are learning opportunities, not career risks
- Being a "teacher-style" leader often makes you a better student

---

## Chapter 7: Sales and Marketing

**Central idea:** Sales and marketing are inseparable, and design leaders are always their firm's best salespeople — because passion drives pipeline.

- Sales and marketing cannot be treated as separate functions — they are linked at every step
- The design leader is the primary marketing and sales representative for the team
- Transparency (sharing process, challenges, insights) is now expected — no more black-box methodologies
- Referrals alone are not enough — build a structured lead-generation process
- Sales is just a series of conversations about a mutually beneficial relationship
- Develop a "sales lens" that matches your firm's focus to filter the right opportunities
- Sales is never done — even when the pipeline is full, keep pushing
- Passion for your work is the most powerful sales tool

---

## Chapter 8: Learning from Our Biggest Mistakes

**Central idea:** Mistakes are inevitable — what separates great leaders is the ability to grow from them rather than avoid or hide them.

- Most project failures trace back to *communication problems* — getting aligned upfront prevents mistakes
- Speak the client's business language, not design jargon
- Approach the business *as* a design project — this makes it more engaging and systematic
- Delegating requires building trust first — trust accelerates delegation
- Don't grow for growth's sake — bigger is not better without purpose
- Use contracts and documentation to make expectations explicit — and as a fallback when things go wrong
- Set realistic expectations; overpromising leads to exhaustion and disappointing everyone
- "Be bolder" — don't be ashamed to put yourself out there and learn publicly

## Cross-Cutting Themes

| Theme | Pattern across the book |
|---|---|
| **People first** | Every chapter returns to: your team is your most important design material |
| **Culture is structural** | It comes from hiring decisions, not perks or slogans |
| **Self-knowledge** | Leaders who know their strengths/weaknesses hire to complement them |
| **Soft skills > Hard skills** | Consistently cited as underdeveloped and high-leverage |
| **Planning ≠ Prediction** | Plans are tools for focus and alignment, not forecasts |
| **Vulnerability as strength** | Sharing mistakes, asking for help, and admitting confusion builds trust |
| **Passion drives performance** | In sales, retention, culture — enthusiasm is a multiplier |
