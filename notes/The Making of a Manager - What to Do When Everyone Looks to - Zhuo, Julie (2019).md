# The Making of a Manager: What to Do When Everyone Looks to You
**Julie Zhuo** | Portfolio/Penguin, 2019

> A memoir-inflected management guide written by Facebook's VP of Design, arguing that management is a craft learned through experience and reflection — not a personality type or a title — and that the manager's only real job is to get better outcomes from the team than the team would produce without them. It matters because Zhuo writes with unusual candor about her own failures and doubts, making it one of the most honest accounts of what early management actually feels like.

---

## Introduction: The Accidental Manager

Zhuo became a manager at 25 at Facebook with no preparation, no training, and no map — and the book is the guide she wishes had existed. The central premise is that great managers are made, not born, and that the making happens in the messy, often uncomfortable work of the job itself.

- Most first-time managers are promoted because they were excellent individual contributors — a skill set with limited direct transfer to management
- The impostor feeling is nearly universal and does not indicate incompetence — it indicates awareness of the gap between current ability and the role's demands
- Zhuo frames the whole book as an answer to a single question: what does it mean to do this job well?

---

## Chapter 1: What Is Management?

The manager's job, stripped to its core, is to get better outcomes by influencing three things: purpose (does the team know what they're trying to achieve?), people (do they have the skills and motivation?), and process (do they have a way of working that lets them succeed?). Everything else is in service of these three.

- Management is not about control — it's about creating conditions for the team to do its best work
- Outcomes, not effort, are the unit of measurement: a team that works hard but misses the goal has not succeeded
- The manager's job looks different at different scales — small teams require hands-on coaching; large teams require building systems and culture

> "The job of a manager is not to do the work yourself. It's to create the conditions for others to do great work."

---

## Chapter 2: Your First Three Months

The first three months establish the patterns — of trust, of expectations, of communication — that define the entire relationship with the team. Zhuo identifies four common entry paths, each with different dynamics and failure modes.

- **Apprentice**: promoted from within; already knows the team; risk is failing to shift from peer to authority
- **Pioneer**: building a new team from scratch; risk is over-hiring or under-defining the mission
- **New Boss**: taking over an existing team as an outsider; risk is moving too fast before understanding the existing culture
- **Successor**: following a beloved or notorious predecessor; risk is over-correcting from the prior leader's style
- In all cases: listen before acting, ask more questions than you answer, and resist the urge to demonstrate authority through early decisions

---

## Chapter 3: Leading Small Teams

Small teams (roughly 3–7 people) are where most managers start, and where the relationship between manager and individual report is most direct. Zhuo argues that trust is the foundation of everything — and that building it takes time and consistency, not grand gestures.

- 1:1s are the most important recurring meeting a manager has — they are relationship-building time, not status-update time
- Critique meetings only function if people feel safe to show imperfect work — psychological safety must be built before critique can be honest
- The highest-leverage early investment is helping each report understand clearly what success looks like for them

---

## Chapter 4: The Art of Feedback

Feedback is the core operating tool of management — and most managers avoid the hard conversations that matter most. Zhuo offers a behavioral framework for giving feedback that separates observation from interpretation.

- Effective feedback is specific, behavioral, and tied to impact — "when you did X in situation Y, the effect on the team was Z"
- Radical candor (from Kim Scott, cited here) — caring personally while challenging directly — is the target; most managers err toward ruinous empathy or obnoxious aggression
- The feedback conversation should feel like a collaboration on how to improve, not a verdict
- Positive feedback matters as much as corrective feedback — specificity turns praise from hollow to instructive

> "The most important thing about giving feedback is to do it. The second most important thing is to do it in a way that actually lands."

---

## Chapter 5: Managing Yourself

The manager who is not managing themselves — their anxiety, their biases, their energy — will eventually transmit those dysfunctions to the team. Zhuo is unusually candid about her own experience of impostor syndrome and her path through it.

- Impostor syndrome doesn't disappear; the goal is to act effectively in spite of it, not to wait until it resolves
- Executive coaching, journaling, and building relationships with peer managers all help calibrate self-perception against reality
- Confidence is not a prerequisite — it's a byproduct of doing the job, getting feedback, and seeing outcomes improve
- Know your own triggers and patterns: the manager who doesn't know how they respond under pressure will be surprised by themselves at the worst moments

---

## Chapter 6: Amazing Meetings

Meetings are one of the primary media through which management happens — and most meetings are poorly designed. Zhuo taxonomizes meeting types by their purpose and argues that each type requires different facilitation.

- Three core meeting types: decision-making, brainstorming, and information-sharing — each has different success criteria and design requirements
- A meeting without a clear purpose and outcome defined in advance is not a meeting, it's a conversation that could have been an email
- The facilitator's job is to make the decision happen, not to make their own view prevail
- Bad meeting culture compounds: one unnecessary recurring meeting teaches the team that meetings aren't valuable, making the next one harder to attend

---

## Chapter 7: Hiring Well

Hiring is the highest-leverage activity available to a manager — one exceptional hire can change a team's trajectory. Zhuo frames hiring as intentional team design, not reactive gap-filling.

- Be specific about what you actually need, not what sounds good on paper — a job description that describes everyone describes no one
- Diverse teams produce better outcomes; this requires designing diversity into the pipeline, not hoping for it at the end
- The interview process should be designed to reveal signal, not to perform rigor — long, exhausting interview loops often produce less useful information than shorter, better-calibrated ones
- Reference calls are the most underused tool in hiring — most managers treat them as formality, but a well-structured reference conversation is often the most honest signal available

---

## Chapter 8: Making Things Happen

Vision, strategy, and execution are not separate phases — they are a continuous cycle that the manager is responsible for connecting. Zhuo uses the origin story of Instagram's explosive growth as a case study in what happens when vision and execution are tightly coupled.

- A clear vision is a prerequisite for good strategy; a good strategy is a prerequisite for effective execution — the manager's job is to ensure the chain is intact
- The manager who can only execute (but not set direction) will always be dependent on someone above them; the one who can do both is ready for more scope
- "Making things happen" means removing the obstacles in the team's path, not adding your own hands to the work
- Inspect the execution loop regularly: are priorities clear? are decisions being made at the right level? is the team moving with appropriate urgency?

> "The most important attribute of a great strategy is that it makes choices. A strategy that doesn't say no to anything isn't a strategy."

---

## Chapter 9: Leading a Growing Team

As teams grow, the relationship between manager and individual becomes more mediated — through managers of managers, through process, through culture. What worked at five people breaks at twenty.

- Delegation is a skill: it requires matching the right task to the right person, giving enough context to succeed, and letting go of the outcome
- The emotional shift from small to large team is significant — you will know fewer people's names, their work, their struggles — and you have to make peace with that
- At scale, the team's culture becomes the manager's primary management lever; individuals can't be directly coached, but culture can be designed
- Hire people who are better than you at the things you're weakest at — insecurity-driven hiring for similarity is one of the most common manager failure modes

---

## Chapter 10: Nurturing Culture

Culture is the sum of the behaviors that are rewarded, tolerated, and penalized — and the manager shapes it more through behavior than through statements. Zhuo ends with the argument that culture is management, not a supplement to it.

- Values are not what's written on the wall; they are what gets rewarded when no one is watching
- Incentives and traditions encode culture — the behaviors the team performs ritually reveal and reinforce what it actually values
- "Walking the walk" is not optional for culture leadership — the manager who says they value candor but punishes disagreement has communicated the actual value
- The most powerful cultural message is what the manager does when it costs them something — when honoring a value is inconvenient or uncomfortable

> "The culture of a team is the behaviors that the team repeatedly exhibits. If you want a different culture, you need different behaviors."
